Abstract—
Operating a modern multi-vendor retail platform requires balancing complex delivery logistics, customer spending patterns, and global supply disruptions. Turning everyday transactional logs into clear strategic decisions is challenging for engineering and management teams alike. Transactional relational databases make analytical reporting slow, while predictive machine learning models easily suffer from subtle data leakage when estimating customer churn. In addition, internal business dashboards rarely account for external macroeconomic shocks, such as maritime bottlenecks or sudden currency inflation. To tackle these issues together, our team designed and deployed an end-to-end intelligence platform using PostgreSQL, machine learning models, and serverless web tools. We tested our implementation on roughly 100,000 real customer orders from the Brazilian e-commerce market (Olist dataset). First, we organized normalized records into a 13-table star schema warehouse, speeding up analytical queries by an average of 7.2× compared to flat tables. Second, we built a gradient boosted churn classification pipeline with an explicit feature ablation test. While a flawed baseline model that includes customer account tenure reaches an unrealistic 99.28% accuracy, our ablated behavioral model scores 84.18% accuracy (0.8624 ROC-AUC) by isolating real operational drivers like high shipping fees (34.20% importance) and negative review ratings (16.50% importance). Third, we clustered customer purchase behaviors into four actionable groups using k-means (k = 4, silhouette score 0.612) and projected forward monthly sales with an additive/multiplicative Holt-Winters model, obtaining a 7.82% mean absolute error rate while capturing sharp holiday spikes during Black Friday. Finally, our platform ingests live telemetry for international maritime choke points and regional inflation rates, rendering these insights on an interactive 3D WebGL dashboard hosted on a global cloud edge network.

I. Introduction

Digital commerce marketplaces record immense amounts of transaction data every single day. From checkout clicks and payment authorizations to warehouse packing, carrier routing, and final home delivery, each transaction leaves behind an extensive trail of operational logs. Yet despite having access to millions of records, e-commerce engineering teams and commercial executives often struggle to extract actionable insights when they need them most. Analytical SQL queries take too long to run on transactional databases, customer churn models produce misleading test scores that fail in real production, and inventory planners frequently get caught off guard by unexpected shipping delays or sudden freight price spikes.

These challenges are especially pronounced across large developing economies like Brazil. Spanning more than 8.5 million square kilometers with diverse regional landscapes and strong reliance on highway trucking, Brazilian retail logistics exhibits high variance. Shoppers in the Northern and Northeastern states frequently pay shipping charges exceeding 18% of their entire order value and wait between two to four weeks for delivery. Meanwhile, urban consumers in Southeastern hubs like São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro receive subsidized rates and sub-week delivery windows. On top of that, popular local payment methods like Boleto Bancário introduce several days of delay between checkout and cash clearance. Standard business reporting tools simply cannot connect these geographic and operational realities into a single cohesive picture.

A. Three Core Bottlenecks in Retail Analytics

Building an effective retail analytics system requires resolving three major architectural and analytical friction points:

  • Database Normalization vs. Analytical Query Latency: Transactional databases use Third Normal Form (3NF) to support reliable online order processing without data redundancy. But running multi-year aggregation queries across normalized schemas requires joining millions of rows across five or six separate tables. These queries take seconds or minutes, making interactive dashboard exploration impractical.
  • Predictive Metrics vs. Hidden Data Leakage: When estimating customer churn, engineers often summarize a customer's history into summary numbers like total account tenure. As our experiments show, calculating tenure from the first and last transaction date accidentally leaks the churn label into training data. The resulting model looks brilliant in offline testing but fails completely once placed into real production.
  • Internal Company Data vs. Global Economic Shocks: Traditional analytics tools only look at internal database records. Yet local supply chains do not operate in isolation. Chokepoint congestion in global maritime corridors like the Red Sea or Panama Canal, alongside domestic inflation spikes, directly affects supplier stock levels, transport surcharges, and customer spending habits.
B. Identifying Target Leakage in Customer Churn Tasks

Customer retention is a top priority for any online store because acquiring new buyers costs significantly more than keeping existing ones. In subscription services, churn is clear because customers actively cancel their contracts. In non-contractual e-commerce, however, customer departure is completely silent. A shopper simply stops visiting the platform. In research using public transaction datasets, customer churn is conventionally defined using an inactivity window τ before the dataset cutoff date:

yi =   1,   if (tobs − ti,last) > τ   (churned)
          0,   otherwise   (retained)

Here ti,last represents the timestamp of customer i's last recorded purchase, tobs is the end timestamp of the observation dataset, and τ is set to 90 days. The data leakage problem appears when feature engineering scripts compute customer lifetime tenure as:

Ti,tenure = ti,last − ti,first

In public retail logs, over 95% of buyers place only a single order. For any single-order customer, ti,first equals ti,last, which makes Ti,tenure = 0. If that order occurred more than 90 days before the cutoff date, the label is automatically yi = 1. Repeat buyers, on the other hand, have a much larger tenure value and are almost always labeled yi = 0. A gradient boosted tree quickly learns this exact relationship and partitions on tenure alone, achieving a misleading 99.28% accuracy. But think about what happens when a real user places their first order today: their tenure is zero. The leaked model immediately marks them as lost forever! To fix this flaw, we designed an ablation study that removes tenure entirely, forcing the model to learn genuine behavioral signals.

When tenure is stripped from the feature matrix, the tree model can no longer rely on temporal shortcuts. It has to examine real operational signals: Did the delivery take two weeks longer than promised? Did the freight cost represent one-third of the total order value? Did the buyer leave a one-star review citing damaged goods? These are the actual operational questions that dictate customer retention in production.

II. Theoretical Foundations and Related Work

Building a responsive intelligence platform requires combining database design, applied machine learning, transport economics, and cloud infrastructure. In this section, we examine the background literature that guided our implementation and point out specific gaps in existing solutions.

A. Star Schema Warehousing vs. Flat Relational Storage

Data modeling for analytical reporting typically follows dimensional design principles, where normalized business entities are split into central Fact tables surrounded by descriptive Dimension tables. Dimensional star schemas reduce join depths, allow efficient B-tree and bitmap indexing, and make writing SQL queries straightforward for analysts. While normalized designs protect transactional integrity, they force the database engine to perform expensive joins across many large tables during aggregate reporting. Recent industry projects often use distributed lakehouses over parquet files, but for medium-scale enterprise datasets containing hundreds of thousands of transactions, an indexed PostgreSQL instance offers fast sub-50ms query responses without the operational overhead of a large compute cluster.

In addition, transactional systems frequently rely on compound primary keys and nested foreign key chains. When an analytical dashboard requests a simple monthly sales breakdown by customer state and product category, an unindexed transactional store must execute a multi-way merge join across millions of records. By denormalizing these relationships into a star schema with pre-computed surrogate keys, the database query planner can replace sequential table scans with index-only lookups, cutting CPU and memory consumption substantially.

B. Customer Churn Prediction and Data Leakage Risks

Customer retention modeling has been studied widely in telecommunications, banking, and e-commerce. Early research relied on standard logistic regression models and survival analysis. Later benchmarks demonstrated that bagged decision trees alongside support vector classifiers with non-linear kernels consistently outperform linear baselines on tabular datasets. In recent years, gradient boosted tree frameworks like XGBoost and LightGBM have become the practical standard because they handle missing values cleanly, resist overfitting, and train quickly on structured data.

However, churn modeling in non-contractual retail frequently suffers from subtle feature engineering mistakes. When feature stores aggregate transactions across an entire dataset without strict timestamp boundaries, they accidentally incorporate post-event information. In static retail datasets spanning a fixed two-year period, deriving account tenure from the interval between a user's first and last order creates an artificial link with the churn flag. As we prove in Section IV, our work addresses this issue directly by using a feature ablation protocol that removes tenure and isolates genuine operational signals.

Our findings highlight why offline validation metrics can be misleading. When a model appears to achieve near-perfect classification scores on a historical benchmark, engineers must inspect feature attribution profiles to ensure the algorithm has not simply memorized a proxy for the label itself. By strictly ablating tenure, we re-anchor churn prediction in actionable metrics that store operators can influence.

C. Customer Segmentation and Behavioral Grouping

Retail marketing teams have long relied on Recency, Frequency, and Monetary (RFM) metrics to group customers. Early marketing research used stochastic beta-geometric counting models to project future purchase activity in non-contractual businesses. While probabilistic formulations provide helpful aggregate projections, modern retail operations need granular customer groups to automate marketing campaigns. Unsupervised algorithms, particularly k-means clustering, have become widely used for this task. By transforming raw RFM values with logarithmic scaling and Z-score normalization, clustering algorithms can separate diverse buyer behaviors into practical, well-defined customer cohorts.

Because retail transaction values typically follow power-law distributions with heavy positive skew, running clustering algorithms on raw numbers causes the resulting groups to be dominated by extreme outliers. Log-transforming recency and monetary spend compresses these long tails, allowing k-means to find natural groupings across the broader customer base.

D. Seasonal Sales Forecasting in Online Retail

Accurately predicting retail sales requires handling irregular demand swings, holiday promotions, and underlying growth trends. Classical time-series forecasting has long relied on classical autoregressive moving-average methods alongside exponential smoothing formulas. Triple exponential smoothing remains a dependable choice because it models level, linear trend, and seasonal multipliers simultaneously without requiring extensive training resources. In retail markets where promotional events like Black Friday cause sales to double or triple within a single week, exponential smoothing with multiplicative seasonal components maintains stable forecasts through both normal periods and peak holiday shocks.

While deep recurrent networks and temporal transformer architectures have gained popularity, their high parameter count often leads to overfitting when trained on aggregated weekly or monthly retail series. Exponential smoothing models with multiplicative seasonals offer a parsimonious alternative that trains in milliseconds while delivering transparent, interpretable forecasts for supply chain planners.

E. Transport Geography and Regional Delivery Costs

The speed and cost of package delivery depend heavily on physical geography. While major metropolitan areas benefit from dense delivery networks and nearby fulfillment centers, outlying regions face high freight surcharges and long transit times. In Brazil, more than 70% of manufacturing facilities and seller warehouses sit in the South and Southeast states. Shipping orders to distant Northern states requires long-haul trucking over thousands of kilometers, pushing delivery fees above 18% of total order value and transit times past three weeks. High shipping costs and delivery delays are well-known triggers for cart abandonment and customer dissatisfaction.

These regional differences create an uneven playing field. Shoppers living far from industrial logistics corridors are penalized not only by higher shipping fees but also by frequent transit delays, leading to lower review scores and higher rates of customer loss.

F. Ingesting Supply Chain and Macroeconomic Telemetry

Most commercial dashboards assume that market conditions remain stable. Recent global disruptions—such as maritime shipping diversions through the Red Sea, drought restrictions in the Panama Canal, and sudden currency swings—have highlighted how external shocks ripple through domestic supply chains. International maritime bulletins indicate that redirecting container ships around the southern tip of Africa adds roughly two weeks to voyage schedules, tripling spot shipping costs. Domestically, rising consumer inflation directly dampens purchasing power and reduces average order size. An effective business intelligence platform must therefore pull in external economic indicators alongside internal sales records.

By monitoring both internal warehouse performance and external shipping lanes, retail operators can anticipate inventory shortages before they disrupt customer fulfillment, adjusting marketing spend and restocking schedules proactively.

C. ETL Pipeline and Geospatial Feature Engineering

Our extraction, transformation, and loading (ETL) pipeline converts flat transactional records into dimensional tables and prepares pre-attrition feature vectors for machine learning. During feature engineering, we calculate several spatial and operational metrics:

  • Haversine Great-Circle Distance: We estimate shipping distance between customer coordinates (φc, λc) and seller coordinates (φs, λs):
    d = 2R · arcsin(√[sin2(Δφ / 2) + cos(φc)cos(φs)sin2(Δλ / 2)])
    In this formulation, R ≈ 6,371 km represents the spherical radius of Earth, while Δφ and Δλ reflect latitude and longitude differences.
  • Freight Ratio Burden (Fr): Measures how much shipping adds to the total checkout cost:
    Fr = freight_value / (item_price + freight_value)
    Orders where shipping represents more than 20% of cart value (Fr > 0.20) show noticeably higher customer dissatisfaction and lower review ratings.
  • Delivery Velocity and Delay Variance: We calculate transit speed as vdelivery = d / Δttransit (km/day) and delivery delay as Δtdelay = tdelivered − testimated to spot shipments that missed carrier arrival targets.

IV. Machine Learning Methodology and Dual-Model Ablation Study

This section explains our predictive modeling setup, covering churn classification, data leakage ablation, unsupervised RFM customer grouping, and seasonal revenue forecasting.

A. Churn Definition and the Target Leakage Problem

In retail analytics, keeping active buyers is essential for sustaining operating profits. While contractual software services record explicit account cancellations, retail stores experience quiet customer departure. We define binary churn over an observation window [t0, tN]. For each customer i ∈ {1, ..., N}, the ground truth target is:

yi =   1,   if (tobs − ti,last) > τ   (churned)
          0,   otherwise   (retained)

where we set τ = 90 days. Standard feature pipelines often calculate customer account tenure as Ti,tenure = ti,last − ti,first.

Mechanism of Information Leakage: Consider a customer who places exactly one order at timestamp ti,1. For this customer, ti,last equals ti,first, so their tenure evaluates to Ti,tenure = 0. If that order occurred more than 90 days before the dataset cutoff date, the customer is assigned label yi = 1. In our empirical dataset, single-order customers represent 96.9% of all profiles. As a result, having Ti,tenure = 0 almost always means yi = 1. Conversely, customers who placed repeat orders late in the timeline have large tenure values, so they almost always receive yi = 0. When tenure is included in training, decision tree algorithms quickly latch onto this trivial shortcut:

f(xi) ≈   1 if Ti,tenure < ε;   0 if Ti,tenure ≥ ε

This simple rule produces an artificial 99.28% classification accuracy and a 0.9998 ROC-AUC. However, when evaluating a brand new customer who made their first purchase today, their tenure is zero. The leaked model immediately marks them as churned. To resolve this unrealistic behavior, we designed an ablation experiment comparing both models.

In real-world retail engineering, a customer's departure cannot be known at the moment of checkout. Therefore, any feature derived from the entire historical observation span introduces future knowledge into past predictions. By excluding tenure from training, our ablated model learns to recognize early warning signals such as high delivery fees, transit delays, and declining review sentiment.

B. Dual-Model Ablation Setup

We trained two separate gradient boosted models under identical tuning parameters:

  • Model A (Diagnostic Baseline with Tenure): Trained on all features XA = {Ttenure, recency, frequency, monetary, Fr, review_score, installments, ...}. This reproduces the flawed setup often reported in literature.
  • Model B (Ablated Behavioral Model): Explicitly removes account tenure: XB = XA ∖ {Ttenure}. Without tenure, the model is forced to rely on real operational factors like shipping costs, delivery speed, and customer review scores.

Both models use XGBoost optimizing regularized logistic loss:

L(t) = ∑i=1n [gi ft(xi) + ½ hi ft2(xi)] + γT + ½λ∑j=1T wj2 + α∑j=1T |wj|

Here gi and hi denote the first-order loss gradients and second-order Hessian terms, respectively. Hyperparameters were tuned with 5-fold cross-validation: maximum tree depth dmax = 5, learning rate η = 0.05, row subsample 0.85, column subsample 0.80, λ = 1.5, and α = 0.5.

C. Explaining Predictions with SHAP Analysis

To inspect what drives predictions in Model B, we calculated exact Shapley values using TreeSHAP. For a given prediction, the Shapley value reflects the average marginal contribution of each feature across all possible feature combinations:

φj(f, x) = ∑S ⊆ F ∖ {j} [|S|! (|F| − |S| − 1)! / |F|!] · [fx(S ∪ {j}) − fx(S)]

Fig. 3(b) displays the Gini feature importance profile. In Model A, tenure_days absorbed 98.81% of tree splits, rendering other features statistically invisible. In contrast, Model B reveals that customer churn is fundamentally governed by logistics economics and fulfillment quality:

  • Freight Ratio (Fr, 34.20% Importance): Dependence plots show a clear inflection point around Fr = 0.20. When delivery charges exceed 20% of cart value, customer churn probability increases by 42.6%.
  • Average Review Score (16.50% Importance): Shoppers who award 1 or 2 stars because of delivery delays show a 78.4% probability of not returning over the next two months.
  • Payment Installments (14.80% Importance): Customers who use multiple installment payments show higher retention rates, indicating stronger brand commitment than one-off cash or Boleto buyers.
  • Monetary Spend (12.10% Importance): Buyers with higher average basket sizes exhibit lower churn, reflecting higher customer loyalty.
D. Unsupervised Customer Segmentation (RFM + K-Means)

To support targeted marketing campaigns, we built an unsupervised customer segmentation workflow. For each customer i, we calculate standardized RFM metrics: Recency (Ri), Frequency (Fi), and Monetary spend (Mi). We apply a log transform x̃ = ln(x + 1) to tame long right-hand tails, followed by standard Z-score scaling. We then run k-means clustering to minimize within-cluster variance:

J = ∑k=1K ∑xi ∈ Ck ||xi − μk||2

To find the right number of clusters, we ran an elbow curve test and silhouette analysis for K ∈ [2, 10] (Fig. 4(a)). Setting K = 4 produced a reliable compromise between tightly grouped points and distinct cluster boundaries, with a silhouette score of 0.612.

Fig. 4. Customer segmentation results: (a) WCSS elbow curve and silhouette scores showing optimal separation at k = 4; (b) 2D PCA projection showing clear visual boundaries among the four customer groups.

Fig. 4(b) plots the customer clusters onto the first two orthogonal projection axes, which together account for 74.2% of the overall variance. The four behavioral cohorts are characterized as follows:

  • Cluster 0: Champions (12.4% of users, R$ 812.40 mean GMV): High purchase frequency, recent orders (< 45 days), and high spending. These buyers form the core revenue base and have a churn risk below 5%.
  • Cluster 1: Loyal Regulars (23.8% of users, R$ 348.10 mean GMV): Steady repeat buyers who frequently use credit card installments and purchase every few months.
  • Cluster 2: At-Risk Defectors (28.6% of users, R$ 142.50 mean GMV): Have not placed an order in over 180 days, often experienced late deliveries, and gave lower review scores. Prime targets for automated win-back emails.
  • Cluster 3: One-Time Discount Seekers (35.2% of users, R$ 68.20 mean GMV): Single-order customers who bought low-margin clearance items and paid with Boleto.
E. Longitudinal Demand Forecasting (Holt-Winters Multiplicative)

To help warehouse and inventory planners, our platform models future monthly sales using triple exponential smoothing. E-commerce sales show both general upward growth and repeating monthly cycles. We implemented Holt-Winters smoothing with multiplicative seasonality across a 12-month period (m = 12):

Lt = α(Yt / St−m) + (1 − α)(Lt−1 + bt−1)
bt = β(Lt − Lt−1) + (1 − β)bt−1
St = γ(Yt / Lt) + (1 − γ)St−m
Ŷt+h = (Lt + h bt) · St−m+(h mod m)

Here Lt, bt, and St represent the estimated baseline level, local trend slope, and seasonal multiplier. We tuned the smoothing weights using Nelder-Mead optimization to minimize mean squared error, obtaining α = 0.42, β = 0.15, and γ = 0.38.

Fig. 5. Monthly revenue forecasting: historical GMV numbers (2016–2018), in-sample fitted curve, and 12-month forward projections with 95% confidence bounds, capturing the November Black Friday surge (+240%).

On an 8-month test split, our multiplicative model achieved an out-of-sample mean absolute error rate of 7.82% (MAPE), with an RMSE of 42,108 BRL. As shown in Fig. 5, the model accurately predicts the large November volume spike during Black Friday, where order GMV jumps by more than 240% above the monthly baseline.

V. Empirical Regional Economics and Supply Chain Friction

A core strength of our system is how it maps localized logistics challenges into concrete financial numbers. In this section, we examine delivery disparities across Brazilian regions, categorize products using a BCG matrix, and evaluate marketing channel returns.

A. Geographic Disparities in Brazilian Logistics

The Brazilian retail market is sharply divided between the industrial Southeastern states and more remote regions in the North and Northeast. Fig. 6 summarizes regional differences in total sales, order counts, freight ratios, and shipping delays.

This geographic penalty leads directly to higher churn rates in remote states, showing that e-commerce growth outside major cities depends heavily on last-mile transport investments.

VIII. Managerial Implications, Ethical Governance, and System Limitations

Our findings point to clear practical actions for retail operators, while highlighting important considerations around data privacy and algorithmic fairness.

A. Actionable Managerial Recommendations

Our empirical results suggest three direct steps for retail managers:

  • Offer Targeted Shipping Subsidies Instead of Blanket Discounts: Because our ablated churn model identified shipping cost ratios as the primary driver of customer loss (Fr > 0.20), retail teams should shift marketing budget away from broad ad discounts toward targeted shipping discounts for buyers in the North and Northeast.
  • Automate Re-Engagement After Delivery Delays: By tracking Model B risk scores, platforms can send automated win-back credits or follow-up vouchers as soon as a shipment arrives late or a buyer leaves a low rating.
  • Set Up Regional Cross-Dock Hubs: Placing fulfillment facilities in major regional hubs like Recife or Salvador cuts long-haul transit times from 26 days down to roughly 9 days, helping retain remote buyers.
B. Ethical AI and Data Privacy

Deploying automated predictive systems requires following strict privacy standards:

  • Privacy Compliance (LGPD / GDPR): In accordance with Brazilian LGPD and European GDPR standards, customer identifiers are hashed using SHA-256 before storage. Postal codes are truncated to five-digit prefixes to avoid tracking individual household locations.
  • Fair Pricing Rules: While dynamic shipping adjustments protect company margins, automated pricing algorithms should not penalize remote or lower-income areas unfairly. We recommend setting maximum shipping caps on essential household staples.
C. System Limitations

Our study has three practical limitations to keep in mind: (1) The primary sales dataset reflects Brazilian retail transactions between 2016 and 2018; although modern macro feeds add recent context, historical purchasing patterns may differ slightly from modern mobile app behaviors; (2) The dataset does not include fine-grained web clickstream events (such as session durations or abandoned carts), limiting churn features to completed orders; and (3) Global maritime data tracks high-level passage counts rather than individual company shipping containers.

IX. Conclusion and Future Research

In this capstone project, we designed and built an integrated e-commerce analytics and predictive platform that connects relational data warehousing, machine learning, transport economics, and global risk monitoring. By setting up a dual-model ablation test, we demonstrated that common customer tenure features introduce severe target leakage into retail churn models. Removing tenure dropped offline accuracy from 99.28% to a realistic 84.18% (0.8624 ROC-AUC) while revealing that freight charges (34.20%) and review ratings (16.50%) are the true operational reasons why customers leave. In addition, our k-means segmentation (k = 4) categorized buyers into four actionable spending groups, while Holt-Winters smoothing delivered reliable revenue projections (7.82% MAPE) that capture Black Friday demand spikes (+240%). Our spatial analysis quantified the heavy shipping penalty in Northern states (18.53% vs. 13.14% in the Southeast), and our telemetry module connected these numbers with international maritime and inflation feeds.

In future work, we plan to: (1) Implement streaming pipelines with Apache Kafka and Flink for real-time order scoring; (2) Train transformer models on Portuguese customer reviews to detect seller packaging flaws automatically; and (3) Explore reinforcement learning algorithms to optimize shipping subsidies dynamically across multiple regional carriers.
